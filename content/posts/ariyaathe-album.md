We move through our institutional spaces with a pre-programmed acceleration. A college campus operates like a compressed city: thousands of individuals rushing toward exams, deadlines, and social friction, leaving a thick haze of background noise in the periphery. In that relentless motion, we develop a specific kind of functional blindness. We learn what to look at and, more importantly, what to edit out.

We map our days by the shortest routes, the fastest Wi-Fi zones, and the classrooms we need to reach before the bell. Everything else, the service doors, the back alleys, the gaps between concrete blocks where no one ever stops, fades into a gray blur. We become experts at looking past things, partitioning our immediate world into what matters to our schedule and what doesn't.

**Ariyaathe** ("Unknowingly") is an exercise in stopping. It is an attempt to look closer at the physical margins of our daily environment, the neglected corners, the concrete runoffs, and the invisible human mechanics that quietly hold an ecosystem together without ever asking for an audience. It asks what happens when we force ourselves to linger in the spaces we usually treat as empty.

<!-- img: IMG_4406 (Cook at the Sink) -->
![Behind the Mesh](https://i.postimg.cc/K3KVV2Nx/chottu.jpg)

Framed by heavy iron grilles, a canteen cook washes dishes in the dim interior. The physical barrier between the student body and the kitchen is absolute. He exists behind the iron, essential to the daily rhythm of the campus, yet entirely anonymous to the hundreds of people who consume what he prepares and walk away. The bars act as a quiet divider between those who consume space and those who maintain it.

---

<!-- img: IMG_4408 (The Staged Sandal) -->
![Dropped between the walls where nobody walks.](https://i.postimg.cc/MXXhX78R/cherupp.png)

Tucked between narrow concrete walls where human eyes rarely peer, a single sandal sits abandoned in the dirt and debris. It waits in vain for its pair and the owner who likely searched for it once. It marks a dead space, an unintended canvas of textures, dust, and discarded fragments. It registers only as a blur if you are moving too fast, but when isolated, it speaks to the casual transience of everyday life: things are misplaced, broken, and left behind.

---

<!-- img: IMG_4411 (The Concrete Canal) -->
![Where the daily excess runs without an audience.](https://i.postimg.cc/XXXTXf0d/chaal.png)

Far from the main pathways, a concrete drainage canal cuts through the margins of the grounds. It carries away the organic runoff and waste generated by the daily rush, out of sight, tucked safely away behind the architecture of routine. Like the labor that feeds the institution, the waste it produces is neatly compartmentalized into spaces people rarely care to look down into.

---

<!-- img: IMG_4413 (The Walking Cook) -->
![Far From Home](https://i.postimg.cc/tZvw2TF1/bangali.jpg)

Caught mid-stride in the alleyway, a migrant cook takes a rare, quiet moment to breathe away from the relentless heat of the kitchen. Far from his home, his family, and a linguistic landscape he recognizes, his presence is just another passing shadow to the student body. He carries the responsibilities of a life thousands of kilometers away while standing entirely alone in a foreign landscape.

---

<!-- img: IMG_4414 (The Boy on the Ground) -->
![Center Frame, Out of Focus](https://i.postimg.cc/Mfc44SDw/daheem.png)

A lone figure stands completely isolated in the vast, dusty expanse of a sports ground. Even in an open public space ostensibly designed for gathering and shared energy, scale and distance can render a person entirely invisible. He is dead center in the frame, yet completely untethered from everything happening around him.

---

We spend our lives building walls to separate the essential from the expendable, forgetting that the structure only stands because of what we choose to ignore.

We construct our routines around the illusion of self-sufficiency, pretending that the environments we inhabit are self-cleaning and self-sustaining. But every brick, every meal, and every clean pathway rests on people and places that exist beyond the boundaries of our attention.

To look at them is not an act of charity. It is an acknowledgment that they were always there.

These spaces and these lives exist just beneath the threshold of our notice, moving alongside us every day.

**Sometimes, all it takes is stopping.**
